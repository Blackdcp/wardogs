## Quick Answer

A WARDOGS mortar works best as a two-part team: a protected crew and a spotter who can see the target. Establish a supplied firing site, receive a current marker, set the required direction and distance using the live interface, fire a small adjustment, correct from observed impact, and relocate when the enemy begins hunting the position. Do not spend ammunition firing continuously at an old callout.

## Confirmed Facts

- WARDOGS combines infantry, vehicles, logistics, construction, destruction, and indirect-fire options in the same objective battle.
- Mortar value depends on information, supply, placement, and what friendly players do after impact.
- FOBs can support forward logistics and fire-support positions but require continued player effort and protection.
- Three competing teams make static firing positions vulnerable to more than one direction of attack.
- Exact mortar damage, blast radius, ammunition cost, range, construction requirement, and balance remain build-sensitive.

Indirect fire is strongest when the target is fixed, the spotter is accurate, and the firing team can sustain ammunition without exposing itself.

The durable skill is communication. Range and azimuth principles continue to matter even if the interface, weapon values, or construction rules change between beta builds.

## How to Use This Guide

Use **Crew and Setup** before deploying, **Range and Azimuth** for the first adjustment, **Spotting Loop** for corrections, and **Counterplay** when you are receiving fire. Always use the current in-game scale and controls instead of old numerical tables.

## Crew and Setup

Use at least a gunner and a spotter when possible. The gunner handles the weapon, ammunition, and local security; the spotter identifies the target, marks it, watches impact, and reports corrections. A third teammate can protect the site, move supplies, or watch the likely counterattack route.

Choose ground that conceals the crew without blocking the live weapon's firing requirements. Keep some distance from the most obvious FOB core, road, tower, or skyline so one reconnaissance pass does not reveal every team asset. Plan an exit before the first shot and avoid placing the full ammunition reserve beside the most visible piece of equipment.

## Range and Azimuth Principles

Azimuth describes horizontal direction; range describes distance. Start with the current map marker and the live mortar interface, then confirm that both crew members use the same reference. Do not copy an old beta conversion table without checking the present build's map scale, units, zeroing, and sight behavior.

Fire one adjustment round or a deliberately small group when conditions allow. The spotter then reports whether impact is left, right, short, or long relative to the target. Change one component in measured steps, repeat, and only commit more ammunition after impacts are useful. If the target moves, stop and acquire a new solution rather than walking rounds through friendly routes.

## Spotting Loop

A good call includes the target type, map marker, urgency, nearby friendlies, estimated direction and distance, and what result would help the push. “Enemies over there” is not enough. A useful example is a marked rooftop firing into the tower approach, with friendlies waiting outside the blast area and a request for one correction round.

After impact, the spotter gives concise correction and confirms when the target is suppressed, destroyed, abandoned, or no longer worth ammunition. The spotter must also call cease fire before friendly infantry enters the area. Proximity voice, squad channels, marker delay, and changing front lines can all create stale information, so repeat the target identity before every larger volley.

## Applying Pressure to a FOB

Indirect fire can force builders, defenders, or vehicle crews away from exposed structures, but shelling alone does not guarantee that a FOB stops functioning. Supplies, spawn support, routes, and critical assets may remain active. Coordinate the barrage with reconnaissance and a ground or vehicle push that can exploit the disruption.

Target observed activity rather than decorative walls. A delivery route, exposed defensive position, repair area, or clustered defenders can be more important than firing at the center of the base. Because exact structure health and mortar damage can change, this guide does not publish a permanent shots-to-destroy table.

## Relocation and Survival

Every launch can reveal direction, sound, or a likely firing area. Move after a useful fire mission, after enemy reconnaissance appears, when supplies become unsafe, or when the Control Zone shifts beyond the position's value. Keep the crew light enough to displace and avoid relying on one route that an enemy squad can block.

The third faction is another reason not to tunnel on the current target. A mortar team focused on one FOB can be found by the other enemy team. Maintain local observation and do not strip all defenders from the firing site merely because the first impacts were successful.

## Counterplay

When receiving indirect fire, spread out, leave repeated impact points, use hard cover and terrain, and avoid clustering around a terminal, spawn asset, or supply pile. Record the direction and rhythm of incoming rounds. Scouts, aircraft, elevated observers, or a flanking squad can search likely concealed sites and supply routes.

Pressure the system, not only the tube. Remove or distract the spotter, interrupt ammunition delivery, force the crew to defend itself, or make the target move. Smoke and movement can break observation, while a coordinated push during the enemy's correction cycle may reduce accuracy. Counter-battery fire is one option, not the only answer.

## Closed Beta Fire Mission, Step by Step

1. The spotter marks a target and confirms that friendly players are clear of the intended impact area.
2. Read the map rather than estimating from the horizon. Closed Beta players described the small grid as roughly 100m and the large grid as roughly 1km, but those measurements must be rechecked after updates.
3. Use the target marker and map to establish range and azimuth. The gunner repeats both values so the spotter can catch a bad number before firing.
4. Fire one ranging round. The spotter reports a simple correction such as short, long, left or right instead of sending a new target description.
5. Walk rounds onto the target, then use a short controlled volley. Stop when the target moves, friendlies enter the area or the strategic value disappears.
6. Relocate or prepare security after repeated fire. A visible firing pattern invites raids and counter-battery pressure.

## Mortar Ammo and FOB Resources

Community testing during the Closed Beta linked mortar ammunition to the FOB's available ammunition resources. This means a firing team cannot be separated from logistics: confirm the base has the correct resource before spending time on a firing solution, and tell the supply team when sustained fire is planned.

Treat the exact resource cost, ammunition type and resupply interaction as build-sensitive. The durable lesson is the workflow: spotter, map, range, azimuth, ranging shot, correction, controlled volley and resupply. That process survives balance changes better than copying one old table of numbers.

## FAQ

**Can I operate a mortar alone?**
You may be able to use the equipment, but a spotter makes targeting, correction, safety, and ammunition use far more reliable.

**What should the first shot do?**
Confirm the solution. Use observed impact to correct direction and distance before committing a larger fire mission.

**Are mortars always effective against FOBs?**
No. Their value depends on observation, supply, target exposure, enemy counterplay, and a follow-up attack.

**How do I survive enemy mortars?**
Disperse, move from repeated impacts, break spotting, identify likely firing routes, and pressure the crew or its logistics.
